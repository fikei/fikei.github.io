Framing

Livongo sold five chronic condition programs — diabetes, hypertension, weight management, behavioral health, and musculoskeletal — in dozens of combinations. Every combination changed which registration fields a member saw. The rules for 50 client configurations across 74 possible fields lived in a spreadsheet that operations cross-referenced manually for every new launch.

The registration flow was built as if every member was starting from scratch. Nine steps. Thirty-five questions. No awareness of what the system already knew about the person signing up.

The data told the story. Every step bled members, but the reasons weren't what anyone expected.

Product selection was the most surprising — 21% drop-off at a step where the system already knew which programs the member qualified for. The product was asking people to make decisions the system had already made.

The Shift

The org measured success by enrollment — each signup triggered 6 months of guaranteed revenue. But the data showed a bigger prize hiding behind it. Activation — 6+ blood sugar checks in 3 weeks — drove 12-month retention and 18 months of revenue.

Registration wasn't just a conversion funnel. It was the first moment a member decided whether to trust the product with their health data. Building trust at onboarding was the lever for durable growth, not just completion.

That reframe changed what the flow optimized for. Instead of minimizing fields, we asked: what does each field need to earn trust? Three patterns emerged — fields the system could skip entirely, fields it could pre-fill for confirmation, and fields where it had to ask but with context about why.

Deliverable

The redesigned flow went from 9 steps to 6. But the step count wasn't the point — what changed was what happened inside each step. The system now resolved data upstream and adapted the flow per client configuration.

Step 1 — Identity reduced to 3 fields. Welcome banner names the employer.
Step 2 — Eligibility auto-confirmed. "We found you!" replaces 3 manual steps.

Program setup with pre-filled records

The blue dots mark pre-filled fields — data sourced from health records and employer HR. The "From Your Records" accordion shows 7 fields the system already knows: diabetes type, diagnosis year, medications, exam dates.

The member reviews and corrects. They don't re-enter. This single change addressed the 21% product selection drop-off — the system now makes program decisions automatically based on the client's Salesforce configuration.

Step 4 — Health history pre-filled from records. Blue dots show sourced data.
Step 5 — Shipping address from employer HR. Confirm, don't enter.

Completion as a beginning

The final screen reframes enrollment as the start of a relationship, not the end of a form. Three concrete next steps — download, meet your coach, set up your kit — bridge the gap between registration and first use.

This is where the activation metric lives. Everything in the flow is designed to get the member to this screen with enough trust and context to take the next step on their own.

Process

Why was product configuration the root cause?

Each program bundle changed which fields appeared, which were required, and which qualification rules applied. The spreadsheet couldn't scale. New bundles broke old assumptions. Registration errors weren't UX problems — they were configuration problems surfacing as bad member experiences.

Why did more context increase completion?

The org assumed shorter = better. But members weren't dropping off because of length — they were dropping off because they didn't understand why they were being asked. Transparency banners, data source attribution, and partnership-oriented copy gave people a reason to keep going.

Why reframe onboarding as trust, not completion?

Enrollment drove 6 months of revenue. But activation — 6+ blood sugar checks in 3 weeks — drove 12+ month retention and 18 months of revenue. The org bias was "less is more" and "completion, not context." But for someone newly diagnosed, onboarding is the first trust moment. Partnership over authority.
